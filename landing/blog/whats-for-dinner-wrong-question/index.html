Why "What's for Dinner?" Is the Wrong Question

Dinner gets easier when you stop searching the whole world of recipes and start from the food that needs eating.

By leftovr 6 min read Updated Nov 10, 2026
Short answer
"What's for dinner?" is hard because it has no limits. Ask "what do we have that needs eating?" instead. That shrinks the choice to a few real options built around food that will not last, so you cook a meal from what is on hand and waste less.

Key takeaways

  • "What's for dinner?" has no edges, which makes the choice feel heavy when you are tired.
  • "What do we have that needs eating?" turns dinner into a short matching problem.
  • Pick one soon-to-spoil item, one filling base and a flexible format like stir-fry or soup.
  • One small purchase to complete a meal beats starting from an empty plan.
  • Starting from what is closest to going off also targets the main source of food waste, which is households.

The better question is "what do we have?"

"What's for dinner?" is the wrong question because it asks you to invent a meal from nothing. The better one is "what do we have that needs eating?" It swaps an open-ended search for a short list, and it turns dinner into a matching problem instead of a creative one. That is why it feels easier, and why it wastes less food.

Why "what's for dinner?" is so hard at 6 pm

The question has no edges. Any cuisine, any recipe, any ingredient is a valid answer, and you are tired. Most people know the feeling: you open a recipe site, scroll for twenty minutes and end up ordering food.

It also hides a second problem. The question does not mention the food you already own. A recipe you pick may need three things you lack, while chicken, half a cabbage and some rice sit in the fridge unused. You solve the creative part and create a shopping problem.

What the right question changes

Asking what you have narrows dinner to a handful of real options. Compare the two:

"What's for dinner?""What do we have that needs eating?"
Starting pointAnything you could imagineThe food in the kitchen
Number of optionsThousandsA few, shaped by what you own
ResultOften a shopping trip or takeawayUsually a meal from what is on hand
Effect on wasteNone, or worseThe oldest food gets used first

Constraints make cooking faster. A cook with an onion, eggs and leftover rice does not agonise. They make fried rice.

Four steps to ask it properly

1. Look for the clock, not the craving

Open the fridge and find the one or two things that will not last: soft produce, open dairy, meat with the nearest date. Those are tonight's starting points.

2. Add a base

Pair them with something filling that keeps, such as rice, pasta, potatoes, bread or tortillas. Now you have a main ingredient and a base. That is already most of a dinner.

3. Pick a format, not a recipe

Stir-fry, soup, frittata, pasta, tacos, a grain bowl and a tray bake all take whatever you have. Choosing a format takes ten seconds, and the exact ingredients can change.

4. Allow one small purchase

If one item would complete the meal, such as a lemon or a tin of tomatoes, buy it. One missing ingredient is a better problem than an empty plan.

Try it tonight

Before you look at any recipe, say out loud one thing in the house that needs using and one filling base. If you can name both, you already have a dinner and only need a format.

Three objections, answered

"But I do not know what I have."

That is usually true, and it is the real reason dinner feels hard. A two-minute look, or a quick photo of the shelf, fixes it. You do not need a perfect list, only the few things that are closest to done.

"We will eat the same things."

Less than you would expect. A stir-fry with cabbage and eggs on Tuesday and one with peppers and tofu on Thursday are different dinners. The format repeats, the contents do not, and repeating a few easy formats is what makes weeknights manageable.

"I like trying new recipes."

Keep doing it. Pick a new recipe for the weekend and use the "what needs eating" question for the weeknights, when time and energy are short.

Why this also helps with waste

Starting from the food that is closest to going off puts the right items first. This matters at household level. The UN Environment Programme's Food Waste Index Report (2024) estimated that households generated about 60 percent of the food wasted globally in 2022. Much of that is food that was bought, not planned for and forgotten.

A habit of asking "what needs eating?" is a small change that attacks that pattern directly. For the planning side, see meal planning from what you already have.

When this approach is not enough

Asking what you have works when there is food in the house. If the kitchen is nearly empty, the honest answer is a shopping trip, and a plan-first approach may suit you better. If you cook for guests or follow a strict recipe on purpose, you will start from the recipe instead. Frequently asked questions

Why is deciding what to cook for dinner so stressful?

The question has almost no limits, so every option is valid and choosing feels heavy, especially when you are tired. Narrowing it to what is in your kitchen cuts the options to a few real ones.

How do I decide what to make with what I have?

Find the item that will not last, pair it with a filling base like rice or pasta, and pick a flexible format such as a stir-fry, soup or frittata. Buy at most one item to complete it.

What should I ask instead of what's for dinner?

Ask what you have that needs eating. It starts from the food that is closest to spoiling and gives you a starting point instead of a blank page.

Does cooking from what I already have reduce food waste?

Yes. It puts the food closest to going off first. The UN Environment Programme reported that households generated about 60 percent of global food waste in 2022, so household habits matter.
