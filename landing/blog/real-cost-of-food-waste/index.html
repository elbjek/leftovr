The Real Cost of Food Waste for Your Household

Food waste is easy to ignore because it is spread across many small bin trips. Added up, it is a real line in the budget.

By leftovr 7 min read Updated Nov 10, 2026
Short answer
The cost of food waste is the price of food you bought and never ate. UK research puts it near £1,000 a year for a household of four. Your own figure depends on how much fresh food you buy and how often it goes unused.

Key takeaways

  • Wasted food is money already spent. Research for a UK household of four puts it near £1,000 a year.
  • The cost also includes the energy, water and effort used to produce the food.
  • Fresh produce, bread and leftovers are the usual big-ticket items.
  • You can estimate your own cost with a four-week toss log.
  • Cutting waste is a saving you can see on the next grocery bill.

How much does food waste cost a household?

For a UK household of four, about £1,000 a year. WRAP, the UK waste charity, estimated that 2021/22 household food waste cost householders £17 billion, which is £250 per person or £1,000 for a family of four. That is the purchase price of the food, before any other cost.

Prices and diets differ from place to place, and not everything thrown out was avoidable, so treat the figure as a sense of scale, not a forecast for your own home.

What is the cost at national scale?

It runs to hundreds of billions of dollars. The USDA Economic Research Service estimated that 31 percent of the 430 billion pounds of food available at retail and consumer levels in the United States in 2010 went uneaten. That was about 133 billion pounds and roughly $161 billion at retail prices. The data is from 2010, so today's dollar figure would be higher.

Globally, the UNEP Food Waste Index Report 2024 found that about 1.05 billion tonnes of food was wasted in 2022, with households responsible for about 60 percent.

What does the cost include?

Three things, only one of which shows on your receipt.

  • Money: what you paid for food you did not eat.
  • Resources: the land, water, fuel and labour behind that food, all spent for nothing.
  • Effort: the shopping, carrying, storing and finally binning.

Only the first is easy to measure at home, so that is where to start.

Which food costs you the most when it is wasted?

The expensive items that spoil fast. A wilted bag of salad costs little. A forgotten piece of fish or a block of cheese costs more. Typical high-cost waste:

Meat and fishBought without a planFreeze the day you buy
Fresh produceBought in bulk, spoils quicklyBuy less, eat first
Bread and bakeryGoes stale in daysFreeze sliced
Cooked leftoversForgotten in the fridgeLabel with a date or freeze
DairyOpened and not finishedSmaller sizes

How do you work out your own number?

Keep a toss log for four weeks. Every time you throw food away, write down what it was and roughly what it cost. Use a note on your phone. Do not change your behaviour for the first two weeks, because you want an honest baseline.

  1. Write the item and the price you paid, or a fair guess.
  2. Add up the total at the end of each week.
  3. Multiply the four-week total by thirteen for a yearly estimate.
  4. Circle the category that shows up most.

Most people are surprised by one thing: the log is mostly a handful of repeat offenders, not a hundred different foods.

Count what you saved too

Tossing is half the picture. When you cook an ingredient that was close to going off, you saved its price. Tracking both sides shows whether your habits are really changing.

Why does the cost feel invisible?

Because it is paid in small pieces. A £3 bag of salad, half a loaf, a tub of leftovers. None of it feels like a loss when it happens. It only becomes visible when you add it up, which is why a short toss log changes behaviour so quickly.

It also hides inside the grocery bill. If you are trying to cut spending, the cheapest saving is often the food you were going to buy again next week because the last lot went off.

Is it worth changing habits for the money alone?

Usually yes, because the change costs nothing. You do not need to buy anything or follow a strict plan. Buying a little less fresh food, eating the oldest first and cooking from what you own all reduce the bill and the bin together.

What is a realistic saving?

Expect to cut the bin, not eliminate it. Some waste is unavoidable: peels, bones, the odd forgotten carton. Most households can shave off a visible slice by fixing their top one or two categories. Do not chase zero. Chase the repeat offenders in your log, and re-run the log after a month to see the change.

If you share a kitchen, run the log together. Everyone sees the same number, and arguments about who forgets the food turn into a plan for fixing it.

What to do with the number

Aim at the biggest category first. If it is produce, buy smaller amounts more often. If it is leftovers, give each one a next use. Our guide on how to stop throwing away food has seven habits to start with, and shopping smarter covers the buying side.

Frequently asked questions

How much money does the average family waste on food?

UK research from WRAP put the purchase cost of wasted household food at about £1,000 a year for a family of four in 2021/22. Your own figure depends on what you buy and how much goes unused.

How do I calculate how much food I waste?

Keep a toss log for four weeks. Note each item and its approximate price, total each week, then multiply by thirteen for a yearly estimate.

What food is wasted the most?

Fresh produce, bread and cooked leftovers are the usual top categories, but your own log will show your pattern. Track it for a month before assuming.

Does food waste matter beyond saving money?

Yes. Wasted food also wastes the land, water, energy and labour used to produce it.
